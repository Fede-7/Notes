• Goal: ensure that data is delivered correctly, completely, and in the original order. 

• Challenge: the underlying communication channel may corrupt bits or lose packets. 

• Abstraction: provide upper layers with a reliable communication service, even when the underlying channel is unreliable. 

• Scope: a fundamental problem at the link, transport, and application layers, not specific to TCP. 

Key question: How can a reliable data transfer protocol be built on top of an unreliable channel?

# Transport Layer Reliable Data Transfer

• Error control (e.g., through checksum) allows receiver at least to understand if the message is corrupted, but this is not enough. Reliable data transfer is still one of the main problem of networking. 

• The problem is not only addressed at the transport layer, but also at the link layer and (often) at the application layer. 

• In a reliable channel 3 elements must be guaranteed: 

1. None of the transmitted bits are corrupted (flipped from 0 to 1, or vice versa). 

2. None of the transmitted bits are lost or repeated. 

3. All bits are delivered in the exact order in which they were sent. 

• In general, we must assume the lower-level network layer to be unreliable. 

• This is a realistic assumption, for example, TCP is a reliable data transfer protocol that is implemented on top of an unreliable (IP) end-to-end network layer.

## What an ARQ protocol needs

• Error detection: extra bits that let the receiver notice the message changed — the checksum you have already met.

## Numbered ACKs make NACKs Unnecessary

• A NACK requests a retransmission. 

• A numbered ACK identifies the last successfully received packet. 

If the sender receives an ACK for the previous packet, the current packet has not been acknowledged. 

The receiver can respond to both corrupted and duplicate packets by repeating the last ACK. 

Key idea: ACKs can provide both positive and negative feedback, eliminating the need for explicit NACKs.

# Transport Layer Reliable Data Transfer: Go-Back-N

• Go-Back-N (GBN) protocol (aka sliding-window protocol): the sender is allowed to transmit multiple packets (when available) without waiting for an acknowledgment but is constrained to have no more than N unacknowledged packets. 

![image](https://cdn-mineru.openxlab.org.cn/result/2026-11-01/745be330-00e3-46e8-a59d-894fc7954a4b/c8c2867693c7f53ec7f72911bc27f3c0b7ff1711befcab34f060dc411ab5f600.jpg)


• Base: is the sequence number of the oldest unacknowledged packet. 

• Nextseqnum: is the smallest unused sequence number (next to be sent). 

• We have that: 

• Packets in [0, base-1] have been transmitted and acknowledged. 

• Packets in [base, nextseqnum-1] have been sent but not yet acknowledged. 

• Packets in [nextseqnum, base+N-1] can be sent. 

• Packets in [base+N, +inf] cannot be used until a new acknowledgment is received.

## Transport Layer Reliable Data Transfer: Go-Back-N - receiver

The receiver keeps one number: 

• Its entire state is expectedseqnum: the sequence number of the next packet it is willing to deliver 

• Correct and in order: deliver it to the application, acknowledge it, move on by one. 

• Anything else: discard it, and re-acknowledge the last in-order packet received. 

• No buffer: A perfectly good packet that arrives early is thrown away exactly as if it had been damaged. 

The complexity is deliberately all on one side: the sender keeps a window, the receiver keeps an integer. 

# Transport Layer Reliable Data Transfer: Go-Back-N - receiver

• The receiver has simply to discard out-of-order packets (whether they are damaged or not) and to deliver to the upper level (app) in-order packets only. 

• Discarded packets (not acknowledged) will be eventually retransmitted by the sender. 

• With this approach good packets are also discarded but the overall process is quite simple: 

• the sender must maintain the indices of the window, 

• the receiver only needs to maintain the sequence number of the next in-order packet.

## Transport Layer Reliable Data Transfer: Selective Repeat

## Acknowledge what is already below your window

• The receiver has that packet, delivered long ago. Why answer at all? 

• Because the sender does not know. If the acknowledgement for the oldest unacknowledged packet was lost, the sender will keep retransmitting it, and its window will never move forward. 

• The two sides do not share a view of what has arrived, and in selective repeat that gap is permanent, not temporary. 

• Silence is never an acceptable answer to a retransmission: it is indistinguishable from a second loss. 

• So the receiver re-acknowledges and drops.

## Transport Layer Selective Repeat: Maximum Window Misalignment

• The sender can transmit at most N unacknowledged packets. 

• If all N packets are received but their ACKs are lost, the receiver advances by N positions while the sender remains blocked. 

• The receiver cannot advance further, since the sender cannot transmit new packets without receiving ACKs. 

• Therefore, the maximum window misalignment is N positions.
